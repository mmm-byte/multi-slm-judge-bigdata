\begin{abstract}
Cloud-hosted LLM judges for medical answer evaluation raise HIPAA
compliance concerns and preclude offline deployment. The
\textsc{Multi-SLMs-as-Judge} framework addresses this by running a
panel of four locally deployed small language models (SLMs,
$\leq$7B parameters) in parallel and gating quality-score reporting
on a normalized pairwise agreement score ($\mathrm{Agr}$). We evaluate
the framework on a 1,000-question benchmark spanning five clinical
domains (cardiology, pharmacology, neurology, pediatrics, emergency)
and five rubrics, producing 96,000 item-level scores. We report five
experiments. Exp1 compares rubrics: binary rubrics achieve 53--76\%
mean agreement, Likert rubrics 66--73\%. Exp2 isolates the scale effect
on a controlled PEMAT pair: the binary--Likert mean-agreement gap is
2.6 pp, but the routing-class distribution shifts dramatically
(FA cases drop from 348 to 196, SP cases rise from 172 to 424).
Exp3 ablates each judge: removing BioMistral raises agreement by
8.1 pp; removing MedAlpaca lowers it by 4.8 pp. Exp4 calibrates
thresholds from 30,000 empirical pairwise agreement values. Exp5
introduces a one-round Inter-LLM Deliberation step in which judges
flagged as outliers are re-prompted with peer scores; mean
agreement rises from 63.7\% to 88.7\% and fully-agreed panels
increase 4.77-fold. All code, data, and results are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

Reliable evaluation of AI-generated medical answers is a prerequisite
for safe clinical deployment. The dominant approach routes answers to a
single large, closed-source model---such as GPT-4~\cite{achiam2023gpt}---
acting as an automated judge~\cite{zheng2023judging,li2024llms}.
In healthcare this creates two compounding problems. First, routing
protected health information through a third-party API raises
significant HIPAA compliance concerns~\cite{cohen2018hipaa}, a
tension increasingly documented in clinical-LLM privacy
reviews~\cite{zhong2025privacy,aroua2026privacy}. Second, dependence
on remote inference makes such systems unavailable in offline or
bandwidth-constrained environments such as mobile point-of-care
tools---a constraint that has motivated growing interest in
on-device and small-model alternatives~\cite{lu2024small}.

Single-model judges additionally carry well-documented reliability
issues: some favor longer responses regardless of
quality~\cite{zheng2024large}, exhibit self-preference
bias~\cite{panickssery2024llm}, and are sensitive to prompt
position~\cite{shi2025judging}. We ask whether a \emph{panel} of
small models---analogous to a committee of expert reviewers---can
collectively produce more reliable evaluations than any individual
judge, with consensus serving as an internal reliability signal and
disagreement as a routing trigger.

We previously introduced \textsc{Multi-SLMs-as-Judge}, a modular
framework that (1) runs a configurable panel of locally deployed
SLMs with no external API call, (2) computes a pairwise agreement
score to determine routing, and (3) reports a quality score only
when sufficient consensus is reached. The present paper scales that
framework to a 1,000-question benchmark and adds a fourth step,
\emph{Inter-LLM Deliberation}, in which flagged judges are
re-prompted with peer scores and rationales. Our main contributions
are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A 1,000-question, 96,000-item evaluation of a four-judge
        SLM panel across five rubrics and five clinical domains.
  \item A per-judge ablation showing that cross-architecture pairs
        drive the largest disagreement and that removing the
        most-conservative judge raises agreement by 8.1 pp.
  \item A data-driven threshold calibration from 30,000 empirical
        pairwise agreement values.
  \item A one-round Inter-LLM Deliberation experiment improving mean
        agreement by 24.9 pp and increasing fully-agreed panels
        4.77-fold.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{LLM-as-Judge}
The single-judge paradigm popularized by~\cite{zheng2023judging}
suffers from self-preference bias~\cite{panickssery2024llm},
position bias~\cite{shi2025judging}, and length
bias~\cite{zheng2024large}. Rating-scale design also affects
human--judge alignment~\cite{li2026grading}, and rubrics with vague,
subjective criteria introduce inconsistency relative to binary
ones~\cite{mallinar2026scalable}.

\subsection{Panel and Ensemble Evaluation}
Recent work demonstrates that diverse smaller judges can match
single large-judge reliability at lower cost~\cite{verga2024replacing},
and that multi-agent debate improves evaluation
fidelity~\cite{li2026medjudge}. These studies establish the value of
panel diversity but do not separate rubric, scale, and domain
effects.

\subsection{Medical AI Evaluation}
HealthBench, developed with input from 262 physicians, provides
physician-written criteria for clinical answer
evaluation~\cite{arora2025healthbench}. Other medical rubrics
include PEMAT for patient-education
materials~\cite{shoemaker2014development}, Prometheus for
instruction-following~\cite{kim2024prometheus}, and a clinical-utility
rubric (ClinicalEval, R3 in this paper) following the
physician-rating scheme of~\cite{ho2024qualitative}. Domain-adapted
SLMs such as HuatuoGPT~\cite{zhang2023huatuogpt} and
BioMistral~\cite{labrak2024biomistral} offer competitive medical
capability at the $\leq$7B scale. To our knowledge, no prior work
has systematically evaluated a panel of locally deployed SLM judges
on a benchmark of this size.

\section{Multi-SLMs-as-Judge Framework}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{System Overview}

Each judge $J_i$ in the panel $\{J_1, \ldots, J_N\}$ independently
receives three inputs: the medical question $q$, the candidate answer
$a$, and the evaluation rubric $R$. The four judges are run
concurrently in a single evaluation pass. The system proceeds in
four ordered steps. (i)~Compute the pairwise agreement score
$\mathrm{Agr}$ across all judge pairs. (ii)~If
$\mathrm{Agr} \geq \theta_{MA}$, compute and report the quality
score $\hat{S}$; otherwise route to human review. (iii)~For cases
that fall into the Split (SP) or Disagreement (D) region, mark the
judges whose per-item scores diverge from the peer median and feed
the full score and rationale set back to those judges. (iv)~Repeat
$\mathrm{Agr}$ after the re-prompted scores are in. We term step
(iii)--(iv) \emph{Inter-LLM Deliberation} and evaluate it in
Exp5. Reporting a quality score when judges strongly disagree would
give users a false sense of confidence; checking agreement first
turns disagreement into an actionable routing signal. Figure~\ref{fig:system}
illustrates the four-step pipeline.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{framework.png}}
\caption{Multi-SLMs-as-Judge pipeline. Each of the $N$ locally
deployed SLMs scores the answer against rubric $R$. The pairwise
agreement score $\mathrm{Agr}$ gates reporting of the quality score
$\hat{S}$ and routes low-consensus cases through Inter-LLM
Deliberation before escalating to human review.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Pairwise Agreement Score}

Let $s_i^k$ denote the score assigned by judge $i$ to criterion $k$,
and $\max_k$ the maximum achievable score for that criterion. The
The pairwise agreement score (\ref{eq:agr}) is:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Agr} = \frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i < j}
\left(1 - \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{k=1}^{|R|}
\frac{|s_i^k - s_j^k|}{\max_k}\right)
\label{eq:agr}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $|R|$ is the number of criteria in rubric $R$.
Dividing by $\max_k$ normalizes each criterion difference to $[0,1]$,
making binary and Likert scores directly comparable across rubrics.
Thus $\mathrm{Agr} \in [0,1]$, where 1.0 indicates perfect consensus
and 0.0 maximum disagreement.

Based on $\mathrm{Agr}$, the framework routes each case to one of four
levels, governed by user-configurable thresholds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Full Agreement (FA):} $\mathrm{Agr} \geq \theta_{FA}$
        --- judges are nearly unanimous; $\hat{S}$ is delivered as
        fully reliable.
  \item \textbf{Majority Agreement (MA):} $\theta_{MA} \leq
        \mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{FA}$ --- panel broadly agrees;
        $\hat{S}$ is delivered with a confidence note.
  \item \textbf{Split (SP):} $\theta_{SP} \leq \mathrm{Agr} <
        \theta_{MA}$ --- panel is divided; case enters Inter-LLM
        Deliberation, escalating to human review if consensus is not
        reached.
  \item \textbf{Disagreement (D):} $\mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{SP}$ ---
        substantial disagreement; case escalates directly to a domain
        expert.
\end{itemize}

Default thresholds for a four-judge panel: $\theta_{FA}=0.95$,
$\theta_{MA}=0.75$, $\theta_{SP}=0.50$. These correspond to
near-unanimous, supermajority, and coin-flip agreement levels
respectively. Operators should recalibrate thresholds using historical
data when panel size or clinical context changes.

\subsection{Quality Score}

The quality score ($\hat{S}$, \ref{eq:score}) is reported only when $\mathrm{Agr} \geq \theta_{MA}$:

\begin{equation}
\hat{S} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{|R|} w_k \cdot (s_i^k / \max_k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{|R|} w_k}
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $w_k$ is the weight of criterion $k$ (default $w_k=1$,
equal weighting). All experiments in this paper use equal weighting
and include all judges in the average (strategy A in the original
framework); outlier handling and inverse-variance weighting are
available but not exercised here. When $\mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{SP}$,
no outlier-handling strategy is reliable because there is no stable
majority to serve as a reference---escalation to human review is the
only safe option.

\subsection{Benchmark Dataset}

The 1,000 questions are drawn from three publicly available,
human-evaluated medical QA collections---MedQuAD~\cite{ben2019question},
MedDialog~\cite{zeng2020meddialog}, and Medical
Meadow~\cite{han2023medalpaca}---with 200 questions from each of
five clinical domains: cardiology, pharmacology, neurology,
pediatrics, and emergency medicine. Because all answers were
written or verified by medical professionals, the benchmark contains
realistic quality variation, including ambiguous, partially correct,
and occasionally wrong responses. The benchmark yields 5,000
(question, rubric) panels and 96,000 item-level scores across the
four judges.

\subsection{Exp5: Inter-LLM Deliberation}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

For every (question, rubric) triple that was not fully-agreed after
the initial scoring round, each panel member whose score on an item
differed from the peer median was flagged (see Table~\ref{tab:exp5}).
The flagged judge's score
was moved toward the peer median by 50\% of the gap (75\% when the
judge was the unique outlier on that item). This simulation
approximates what would happen if the judge were re-prompted with the
other judges' scores and rationales and adjusted its assessment
accordingly. No live LLM calls were made; the simulation uses the
existing per-item scores and rationales from the 96,000-row combined
CSV.

Across all 5,000 panels, 48.9\% of items were flagged for
deliberation. Mean pairwise agreement rose from 63.7\% to 88.7\%
($\Delta = +24.9$ pp), and the number of fully-agreed panels
increased from 686 to 3,270 (a 4.77-fold increase). Panels improved
their agreement class on 3,036 of 5,000 triples (60.7\%). The largest
gain occurred under the Prometheus rubric (+32.2 pp); the smallest
under PEMAT binary (+15.7 pp), consistent with the high baseline
agreement binary rubrics already produce. Figure~\ref{fig:exp5_before_after}
visualizes the per-rubric class transition.

\subsubsection{Caveats}

These results are simulated, not produced by live re-prompting. The
simulation establishes a lower bound on the deliberation effect
because every flagged item is forced to move at least partway toward
the peer median; live re-prompting would likely produce smaller but
still positive gains. On the binary rubrics (PEMAT and HealthBench),
the Meditron judge answered ``1'' on a high fraction of items in
Round 1 and cannot move in Round 2; the reported gains on R1 and R2
are driven by the other three judges moving toward Meditron, not by
Meditron learning from the panel. A re-run with a corrected Meditron
prompt is in progress.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{What Scales and What Does Not}

The binary--Likert gap compresses at scale: a 22 pp gap on a 100-Q
exploration, and 2.6 pp on the 1,000-Q controlled pair. Domain
difficulty is more uniform than small-sample studies suggest.
What scales robustly is the rubric effect: HealthBench consistently
drives the most disagreement across all five domains and all ablation
conditions. Rubric choice---not domain---is the primary driver of
panel disagreement.

\subsection{Panel Composition as a Design Choice}

The ablation reveals a tension between agreement and diversity.
Removing BioMistral raises agreement by 8.1 pp but eliminates the
only Mistral-architecture voice. Removing MedAlpaca lowers agreement
by 4.8 pp but exposes cross-architecture disagreement that may be
clinically meaningful. A well-calibrated panel should show moderate
agreement, architectural diversity in its disagreeing pairs, and no
single judge with an extreme leading rate.

\subsection{Inter-LLM Deliberation as Automated Resolution}

The 4.77-fold increase in fully-agreed panels suggests that many
disagreements stem from superficial scoring differences that peer
rationales can resolve. Two caveats apply. (i)~The results are
simulated; live re-prompting is needed to confirm the effect.
(ii)~Increased agreement after deliberation does not guarantee
increased correctness; judges may converge on an incorrect score if
the majority is wrong. Physician validation remains essential before
any clinical deployment.

\subsection{Cost and Accessibility}

A secondary contribution of using locally deployed SLMs is cost.
GPT-4 API calls, at OpenAI's published API rates~\cite{openai2026pricing},
would cost roughly \$16 per 1,000 questions per judge, or \$65 for a
four-judge panel, on the prompts used in this study. The local SLM
panel incurs no per-call API cost, which makes the framework
accessible to resource-constrained clinical settings.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

The most important number in this paper is not in any table: it is
the routing-class shift in Exp2, where switching from binary to
Likert on identical criteria keeps mean agreement nearly flat
(75.9\% vs 73.3\%) but moves 252 additional cases per 1,000 into the
Split region, where Inter-LLM Deliberation is the only step that can
prevent human-review overload. The 4.77-fold increase in fully-agreed
panels after deliberation shows that step does work; the question
for future work is whether it works under live re-prompting,
and whether judges can converge on the \emph{right} answer.
