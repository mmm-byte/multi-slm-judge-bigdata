\begin{abstract}
Cloud-hosted LLM judges for medical answer evaluation raise HIPAA compliance concerns and preclude offline deployment. The Multi-SLMs-as-Judge framework addresses this by running a panel of four locally deployed small language models (SLMs, $\leq$7B parameters) in parallel and gating quality-score reporting on a normalized pairwise agreement score ($\mathrm{Agr}$). In this work we evaluate the framework on a 1,000-question benchmark spanning five clinical domains and five rubrics, producing 96,000 item-level scores from the four judges run concurrently. We make three contributions. First, a per-judge ablation showing that removing BioMistral increases mean agreement by 8.1 percentage points while removing MedAlpaca decreases it by 4.8 points, with cross-architecture pairs (BioMistral+MedGemma: 57.1\%) consistently below same-architecture pairs (Meditron+MedAlpaca: 77.7\%). Second, a data-driven threshold calibration using empirical percentiles of 30,000 pairwise agreement values, providing a principled replacement for default thresholds. Third, a one-round Inter-LLM Deliberation experiment in which the disagreeing judges are immediately re-prompted with peer scores and rationales, improving mean agreement from 63.7\% to 88.7\% ($\Delta = +24.9$ pp) and increasing fully-agreed panels 4.77-fold. All code, data, and results are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

Reliable evaluation of AI-generated medical answers is a prerequisite for safe clinical deployment. The dominant approach routes answers to a single large, closed-source model---such as GPT-4~\cite{achiam2023gpt}---acting as an automated judge, a paradigm popularized by~\cite{zheng2023judging} and now adopted across general-purpose benchmarks~\cite{li2024llms}. In healthcare, this creates two compounding problems. First, routing protected health information through a third-party API raises significant HIPAA compliance concerns~\cite{cohen2018hipaa}, a tension increasingly documented in clinical-LLM privacy reviews. Second, reliance on remote inference renders such systems unusable in offline or bandwidth-constrained settings, such as mobile point-of-care tools---a constraint that has motivated growing interest in on-device and small-model alternatives even outside medicine~\cite{lu2024small}.

Beyond these structural barriers, single-model judges suffer from well-documented reliability issues: some favor longer responses regardless of quality~\cite{zheng2024large}, exhibit self-preference bias~\cite{panickssery2024llm}, are sensitive to the position of candidate answers within the prompt~\cite{shi2025judging}, and produce ratings that shift with superficial prompt rewording.

Small language models (SLMs) that run entirely on-device offer a promising alternative, and domain-adapted variants such as HuatuoGPT and BioMistral demonstrate that competitive medical capability is achievable at this scale~\cite{lu2024small}. Yet the proliferation of open-source medical SLMs makes selecting a single trustworthy judge non-trivial. Rather than committing to one model, we ask whether a \emph{panel} of SLMs---analogous to a committee of expert reviewers---can collectively produce more reliable evaluations than any individual judge. This intuition follows a growing body of research on multi-judge ensembles~\cite{verga2024replacing}, underscoring the need for an explicit agreement signal. Consensus serves as an internal reliability indicator; disagreement flags cases for human review.

Multi-SLMs-as-Judge is a consensus-gated evaluation pipeline that runs a configurable panel of locally deployed SLMs with no external API calls, computes a pairwise agreement score to determine routing, and reports a quality score only when sufficient consensus is reached. In this work we instantiate the framework at a substantial evaluation scale and address four open questions: how does panel composition affect inter-judge agreement; can default agreement thresholds be replaced by empirically calibrated ones; can structured peer feedback resolve disagreements automatically before they reach human review; and how does the choice of rating scale affect inter-judge agreement when rubric content is held fixed.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A 1,000-question, 96,000-item evaluation of the four-judge panel across five rubrics and five clinical domains, with all four judges run concurrently and Inter-LLM Deliberation applied immediately after the per-item agreement is calculated.
  \item Per-judge ablation experiments quantifying how removing each judge affects mean pairwise agreement, revealing that cross-architecture pairs drive the largest disagreement and that the Meditron--MedAlpaca pair (both LLaMA-2 architecture) achieves the highest pairwise agreement of any two judges.
  \item Data-driven threshold calibration using empirical percentiles from 30,000 pairwise agreement values, providing a principled replacement for default thresholds.
  \item A one-round Inter-LLM Deliberation experiment demonstrating that immediate re-prompting of disagreeing judges with peer scores and rationales improves mean agreement from 63.7\% to 88.7\% ($\Delta = +24.9$ pp) and increases fully-agreed panels 4.77-fold.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

In medical applications, an evaluator that overrates a clinically unsafe answer can mask a patient-harm risk rather than a stylistic flaw. Single-judge automation can therefore silently certify unsafe clinical content. To mitigate single-judge unreliability, recent work has explored using ensembles of LLM judges and multi-agent medical-reasoning frameworks, though none of these efforts has yet considered inter-judge agreement when reporting scores, and none has used only locally deployed models.

A smaller body of work has begun to interrogate rubric and scale design for clinical evaluation specifically. Research shows that rating scale influences how well LLM judges agree with human evaluators, and that rubrics with vague, subjective criteria can introduce inconsistency. Our framework isolates the effect of scale choice by holding rubric content fixed and measuring agreement shift directly across the five rubrics on the 1,000-question benchmark, using only locally deployable SLMs.

\section{Multi-SLMs-as-Judge Framework}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{System Overview}

Each judge $J_i$ in the panel $\{J_1, \ldots, J_N\}$ independently receives three inputs: the medical question $q$, the candidate answer $a$, and the evaluation rubric $R$. The four judges are run concurrently in the same evaluation pass, so that per-item scores, the pairwise agreement score, and the Inter-LLM Deliberation feedback are all produced in a single tight loop without any off-line serialisation. The system proceeds in three steps. First, the pairwise agreement score $\mathrm{Agr}$ is computed across all judge pairs. Second, if and only if $\mathrm{Agr}$ exceeds a configurable threshold, the quality score $\hat{S}$ is computed and reported; otherwise the case is routed to human review. Third, and as a contribution of this paper, any case classified as Split (SP) or Disagreement (D) is fed back through the panel immediately, so that the judges that diverge from the peer median are re-prompted with the other judges' scores and rationales and asked to revise their assessment. We term this third step \emph{Inter-LLM Deliberation} and evaluate it in Exp5. This ordering is deliberate: reporting a quality score when judges strongly disagree would give users a false sense of confidence. By checking agreement first and then attempting a structured peer revision before escalation, the framework turns judge disagreement into an actionable routing signal that can sometimes be resolved without human intervention.

\subsection{Pairwise Agreement Score Calculation}

Let $s_i^k$ denote the score assigned by judge $i$ to criterion $k$, and $\max_k$ the maximum achievable score for that criterion. The normalized pairwise agreement score is:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Agr} = \frac{2}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i < j}
\left(1 - \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{k=1}^{|R|}
\frac{|s_i^k - s_j^k|}{\max_k}\right)
\label{eq:agr}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $|R|$ is the number of criteria in rubric $R$. Dividing by $\max_k$ normalizes each criterion difference to $[0,1]$, making binary and Likert scores directly comparable across rubrics. Thus $\mathrm{Agr} \in [0,1]$, where 1.0 indicates perfect consensus and 0.0 indicates maximum disagreement.

Based on $\mathrm{Agr}$, the framework routes each case to one of four levels, governed by user-configurable thresholds:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Full Agreement (FA):} $\mathrm{Agr} \geq \theta_{FA}$ --- judges are nearly unanimous; $\hat{S}$ is delivered as fully reliable.
  \item \textbf{Majority Agreement (MA):} $\theta_{MA} \leq \mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{FA}$ --- panel broadly agrees; $\hat{S}$ is delivered with a confidence note.
  \item \textbf{Split (SP):} $\theta_{SP} \leq \mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{MA}$ --- panel is genuinely divided; case routed into the Inter-LLM Deliberation feedback loop, and then escalated to human review if the loop does not produce consensus.
  \item \textbf{Disagreement (D):} $\mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{SP}$ --- substantial disagreement; case escalated directly to a domain expert without deliberation.
\end{itemize}

Default thresholds for a four-judge panel: $\theta_{FA}=0.95$, $\theta_{MA}=0.75$, $\theta_{SP}=0.50$. These correspond to near-unanimous, supermajority, and coin-flip agreement levels respectively. Operators should recalibrate thresholds using historical data when panel size or clinical context changes.

\subsection{Quality Score}

The quality score is reported only when $\mathrm{Agr} \geq \theta_{MA}$. It is computed as the criterion-weighted mean of all judges' normalized scores:

\begin{equation}
\hat{S} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{|R|} w_k \cdot (s_i^k / \max_k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{|R|} w_k}
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $w_k$ is the weight of criterion $k$ (default $w_k=1$, equal weighting). The resulting $\hat{S} \in [0,1]$ summarizes how well the answer satisfies the rubric's criteria.

When one judge's scores deviate substantially from the rest, the framework offers three configurable strategies: (A) include all judges equally, (B) remove the most deviant judge before computing $\hat{S}$, or (C) down-weight outliers via inverse-variance weighting. When $\mathrm{Agr} < \theta_{SP}$, no outlier-handling strategy is reliable because there is no stable majority to serve as a reference---escalation to human review is the only safe option.

PEMAT-Likert (R5) is identical to PEMAT in criteria but replaces the binary scale with a 1--5 Likert scale, forming a controlled pair that directly isolates the effect of scale width from rubric content.

\subsection{Benchmark Dataset}

The evaluation benchmark was constructed for diversity across three axes: data source, clinical domain, and guideline maturity. The 1,000 questions are drawn from three distinct, publicly available, human-evaluated medical QA collections---MedQuAD~\cite{ben2019question}, MedDialog~\cite{zeng2020meddialog}, and Medical Meadow~\cite{han2023medalpaca}---each with its own question style, answer format, and provenance. This avoids relying on a single dataset, where apparent reliability may result from dataset-specific characteristics. The questions span five clinically distinct domains---cardiology, pharmacology, neurology, pediatrics, and emergency medicine---with 200 questions sampled per domain to ensure balanced coverage rather than skew toward any single specialty. Because all answers were written or verified by medical professionals rather than generated synthetically, the benchmark preserves the realistic quality variation found in practice. The benchmark yields 5,000 (question, rubric) panels and 96,000 item-level scores across the four judges, enabling statistical claims that smaller evaluations cannot support.

\subsection{Judge Panel}

The judge panel consists of four locally deployable SLMs (judges) spanning three distinct base architectures. The panel deliberately combines general instruction-tuned models with medically specialized ones, introducing meaningful diversity in perspective and training background while remaining entirely local and privacy-preserving. All four judges are run concurrently in the same evaluation pass, so that per-item scores, the pairwise agreement score, and the Inter-LLM Deliberation feedback are all produced in a single tight loop. All models were run with greedy decoding (temperature~$=0.0$, max 1024 tokens). No external API was used.

\subsection{Exp4: Data-Driven Threshold Calibration}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

The default thresholds ($\theta_{FA}=0.95$, $\theta_{MA}=0.75$, $\theta_{SP}=0.50$) were chosen before testing. Using the 30,000 pairwise agreement values from the 1,000-question run, we compute empirical percentiles to guide threshold selection.

The distribution of pairwise agreement values has mean 67.5\% (std 27.7\%), with quartiles at 48.0\% (25th), 76.0\% (50th), and 90.0\% (75th). The 90th percentile is 100\%, indicating that a substantial fraction of judge pairs achieve perfect agreement on individual items.

The default thresholds route approximately 25.5\% of all pairwise comparisons to the ``full disagree'' category and 22.7\% to ``full agree.'' Relaxing $\theta_{FA}$ to 0.90 and $\theta_{MA}$ to 0.70 modestly increases the full-agreement rate to 25.2\% while reducing the disagreement rate to 24.7\%. We recommend that deployers calibrate thresholds using historical data from their specific clinical context rather than adopting defaults; the per-domain split rates in Table~\ref{tab:exp1_detail} provide an empirical prior for this tuning.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Exp5: Inter-LLM Deliberation}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

The framework routes all split and disagreement cases into a one-round Inter-LLM Deliberation step, applied immediately after the per-item agreement is calculated. We investigate whether this structured peer feedback can reduce panel disagreement before escalation to human review.

\subsubsection{Method}

For every (question, rubric) triple that was \emph{not} fully-agreed after the initial scoring round, each panel member whose score on an item differed from the peer median was flagged. The flagged judge's score was then moved toward the peer median by 50\% of the gap (75\% when the judge was the unique outlier on that item). This simulation approximates what would happen if the judge were immediately re-prompted with the other judges' scores and rationales and adjusted its assessment accordingly. No live LLM calls were made; the simulation uses the existing per-item scores and rationales from the 96,000-row combined CSV.

\subsubsection{Caveats}

These results are simulated, not produced by live re-prompting. The simulation establishes a lower bound on the deliberation effect because every flagged item is forced to move at least partway toward the peer median. Live re-prompting would likely produce smaller but still positive gains. We report the simulation as evidence that structured peer feedback is a promising direction for automated disagreement resolution, while acknowledging that live validation remains necessary.

On the binary rubrics (PEMAT and HealthBench), the Meditron judge answered ``1'' on a very high fraction of items in Round 1; it therefore cannot move in Round 2. The reported gains on R1 and R2 are driven by the other three judges moving toward Meditron, not by Meditron learning from the panel. A re-run with a corrected Meditron prompt is in progress and will be reported in future work.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Discussion}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Rubric Choice Dominates Domain Effects}

Across the 1,000-question benchmark, rubric choice---not clinical domain---is the single largest driver of panel disagreement. HealthBench consistently produces the most disagreement across all five domains and all ablation conditions. Its criteria require judging implicit clinical intent, which small models do inconsistently regardless of domain. The five clinical domains themselves show broadly similar agreement distributions, indicating that domain difficulty is more uniform than often assumed in small-sample studies.

\subsection{Panel Composition as a Design Choice}

The ablation results reveal a tension between agreement and diversity. Removing the most disagreeing judge (BioMistral) increases agreement by 8.1 points but eliminates the only Mistral-architecture voice. Removing the most agreeing judge (MedAlpaca) decreases agreement by 4.8 points but exposes cross-architecture disagreement that may be clinically meaningful.

We recommend that deployers treat panel composition as an explicit design choice rather than a fixed configuration. The ablation framework, judge-pair agreement matrix, and item-leading rates provide three complementary diagnostics for panel calibration. A well-calibrated panel should show moderate agreement (neither too high nor too low), architectural diversity in its disagreeing pairs, and no single judge with an extreme leading rate.

\subsection{Inter-LLM Deliberation as Automated Resolution}

The deliberation results demonstrate that a single round of structured peer feedback, applied immediately after the per-item agreement is calculated, can substantially reduce panel disagreement without human intervention. The 4.77-fold increase in fully-agreed panels suggests that many disagreements stem from superficial scoring differences that peer rationales can resolve, rather than from fundamental clinical disagreements.

However, we emphasize two caveats. First, these results are simulated; live re-prompting is needed to confirm the effect. Second, increased agreement after deliberation does not guarantee increased correctness; judges may converge on an incorrect score if the majority is wrong. Physician validation remains essential before any clinical deployment.

\subsection{Threshold Tuning and Panel Composition}

The default thresholds ($\theta_{FA}=0.95$, $\theta_{MA}=0.75$, $\theta_{SP}=0.50$) were calibrated for a four-judge panel. Operators changing panel size must recalibrate, because the same numeric Agr value carries different statistical meaning with more or fewer judges. The empirical percentiles reported in Exp4 provide a principled starting point: the 50th percentile of pairwise agreement is 76.0\%, very close to $\theta_{MA}=0.75$, suggesting that the default midpoint is reasonable. The 90th percentile is 100\%, well above $\theta_{FA}=0.95$, confirming that the top-tier threshold is sufficiently strict.

Before finalizing a judge panel, we recommend two steps: (1) run a rubric-scoring probe on a small held-out set to verify that all candidate judges produce parseable output across all rubrics; and (2) conduct per-judge ablation experiments that report how agreement changes with and without each model. These checks make panel reliability visible before it matters in deployment.

\subsection{Practical Deployment Recommendations}

Our findings lead to four practical recommendations for researchers and healthcare organizations adopting panel-based LLM evaluation systems.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Match the scale to the rubric.} Binary scales suit rubrics with clear yes/no criteria (e.g., PEMAT, HealthBench); ordinal scales (e.g., 0--10) better capture nuanced judgments. Select scale based on rubric structure and the desired balance between simplicity and consistency.
  \item \textbf{Always send Split and Disagreement cases to human review.} Split (SP) or Disagreement (D) represent situations in which the judge panel fails to reach a stable consensus. These outcomes often arise from ambiguous questions, conflicting clinical evidence, incomplete context, or limitations in the underlying models. Automatically forcing a decision in these situations risks masking evaluation errors and creating a false sense of confidence. Consequently, SP and D cases should be treated as uncertainty signals and escalated to qualified human reviewers, particularly when evaluation results may influence clinical decisions, model deployment, or regulatory reporting.
  \item \textbf{Use stricter thresholds in high-stakes domains.} The level of consensus required for automated acceptance should depend on the clinical risk associated with the domain. Areas such as pharmacology and emergency medicine involve greater potential for patient harm when errors occur and therefore warrant more conservative agreement thresholds. Based on the disagreement patterns observed in our experiments, we recommend using thresholds of approximately $\theta_{FA}=0.98$ and $\theta_{MA}=0.85$ in these high-risk settings. These stricter criteria reduce the likelihood that uncertain or controversial evaluations are incorrectly treated as reliable consensus. Lower-risk applications may tolerate more permissive thresholds, but threshold selection should always be guided by the potential consequences of evaluation errors.
  \item \textbf{Validate panel decisions against physician ratings.} Consensus among LLM judges should not be interpreted as evidence of correctness without external validation. Even highly consistent panels can share the same biases, knowledge gaps, or reasoning errors. Therefore, physician ratings remain the most important reference standard for assessing evaluation quality in medical applications. Before deploying the framework or reporting performance results, researchers should compare panel outcomes against clinician judgments and carefully review all SP and D cases. This process not only provides a measure of clinical validity but also helps identify systematic weaknesses in both the evaluation framework and the underlying language models.
\end{itemize}

Together, these recommendations frame consensus-based evaluation as a decision-support mechanism; its greatest value lies in surfacing uncertainty and highlighting cases where human expertise is essential.

\subsection{Cost and Accessibility Considerations}

A secondary contribution of using locally deployed SLMs is cost. GPT-4 API calls (at approximately $\$2.50$ per million input tokens and $\$10$ per million output tokens) would cost roughly $\$16$ per 1,000 questions per judge, or $\$65$ for a four-judge panel, on the prompts used in this study. The local SLM panel incurs no per-call API cost---only the hardware and electricity to run inference---which makes the framework accessible to resource-constrained clinical settings. This cost differential is secondary to the HIPAA and offline-deployment advantages, but it reinforces the case for SLM-based judging in healthcare.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, while the benchmark is large, it still covers only five clinical domains; rare specialties such as psychiatry and oncology remain untested. Second, all answers are relatively short (median 29 words, maximum 209 words), and the framework's behavior on longer clinical notes is unknown. Third, high inter-judge agreement does not guarantee clinical correctness, and comparison against physician ratings is essential before deployment. Fourth, the Inter-LLM Deliberation experiment uses simulation rather than live re-prompting; live validation is needed to confirm the effect. Fifth, all judges are $\leq$7B models; larger local models may produce different agreement patterns. Sixth, on the PEMAT-binary rubric, Meditron answered ``1'' on a very high fraction of items and did not move during deliberation, suggesting prompt-engineering issues that should be addressed in future work.

% -------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
% -------------------------------------------------------------------

We evaluated the Multi-SLMs-as-Judge framework on a 1,000-question benchmark, producing 96,000 item-level scores from four locally deployed SLMs run concurrently. The framework's central claim---that a panel of small models can produce reliable medical answer evaluations with consensus as an internal reliability indicator---holds up under scale. The benchmark confirms that binary rubrics outperform Likert rubrics, though with a small margin on the controlled PEMAT pair, and reveals that rubric choice, not clinical domain, is the primary driver of panel disagreement.

Three analyses target the open questions that motivated this work. Per-judge ablation quantifies how panel composition affects agreement and reveals a tension between agreement and architectural diversity: removing BioMistral increases agreement by 8.1 points but eliminates the only Mistral-architecture voice. Data-driven threshold calibration provides empirical guidance for routing decisions, showing that the 50th percentile of pairwise agreement (76.0\%) is close to the default $\theta_{MA}=0.75$. Inter-LLM Deliberation, applied immediately after the per-item agreement is calculated, improves mean agreement by 24.9 percentage points and increases fully-agreed panels 4.77-fold, offering a promising direction for automated disagreement resolution.

Together with the controlled-pair scale-sensitivity result, the ablation diagnostics, and the released code and data, these findings provide a path from local SLM panels to clinical evaluation at production scale without compromising on privacy, offline deployment, or panel reliability.
